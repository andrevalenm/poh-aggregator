Three places this is needed

Keeping bots out of social apps

Reddit, Discord, Bluesky, every forum with a comment box. They need to know an account is a distinct person, and they need pseudonymity to survive the check. Nobody uploads a passport to post on a subreddit, and no platform should want that file sitting on its servers. This is the case KYC cannot serve at any price.

Stopping scalpers on ticket drops

Ticketmaster presales, sneaker drops, anything scarce and first-come. Professional operators run thousands of accounts against the queue, and every second of friction you add to stop them costs you a real buyer. It is the airdrop problem wearing a suit, and it is decided in the seconds before a page loads.

Knowing who is behind an AI agent

Agents already transact on their own. Before you deal with one you need to know a person stands behind it, and whether it is that person's fourth. On World's AgentBook today, 830 humans have registered 1,164 agents, and one of them runs twenty-seven. Every one of those twenty-seven passes “is there a human”.

Three protocols that taught us something

Case 01 — Idena

Idena did the hard thing. It filtered bots with a synchronous cognitive ceremony, and — uniquely — it beat account trading, using identity staking and periodic re-authentication. It still collapsed. Not into sybils: into puppets. Real, verified, unique humans paid a few dollars an epoch to perform ceremonies while an operator held the keys and took the rewards.

  • Peak network, Apr 202215,778 accounts
  • Human identities, Jul 202698
  • Large pools, Jun 2021 → May 202222% → 61%
  • 23 entities (<0.6%) controlled~40% of accounts
  • The bribe that did it$2–$14 / epoch

Two findings changed our design. First, the operators' on-chain signature — simultaneous one-way transfers to one wallet — is identical for puppeteering and for voluntary cooperation. They differ only in the off-chain distribution of power, so no amount of chain analysis can separate them. Someone even shipped a forked client whose purpose was to hide a user's own private key from them.

Second, the accident that saved the network. Voting was one-node-one-vote and pools had consolidated onto single nodes, so by May 2022 large pools held 61% of accounts but only ~2.4% of votes, while solo accounts held 27% of accounts and ~89% of votes. An unintended correlation discount let the honest minority fork the protocol out of the crisis. That is the single most constructive datum in the literature for anyone building a scoring system, and it is why we discount correlation rather than try to detect fraud.

The fix that came next is a warning of its own. Sublinear identity staking (IIP-5, stake^0.9) did kill the puppet pools — and human identities fell 97.6% from the fork to today. Concave weighting is a tax on your honest users too, and the exponent decides who leaves.

Case 02 — Circles

Circles is the only genuinely biometric-free, document-free trust root in the landscape: no passport, no iris, no liveness check anywhere in the protocol. Every registered human mints their own token at 1 CRC/hour, and a public directed trust graph decides whose money you will accept.

Which puts the weight on the graph rather than the registration, and the arithmetic is in Hub.sol: an invite burns 96 CRC from the inviter and mints a 48 CRC welcome bonus to the invitee. At 24 CRC/day of issuance that is a net two days of freely-minted currency per new node, plus gas. A rate limiter with a two-day doubling time, not a sybil barrier.

  • v2 humans (Jul 2026)26,040
  • v2 trust edges~473,000
  • Cost to add a node~2 days of CRC
  • Documents / biometrics requirednone

The FAQ's “one human, one account” is a norm, not an invariant — enforced by the team's discretion, with no technical mechanism in the contract. There is on-chain evidence of a 5,000-account farm run by a single maintainer.

So we do not score the boolean. The signal is position in the trust graph, and the whitepaper supplies the right metric itself: max-flow to a seed set. That is a traversal over a large event history — a question about relationships, not a storage slot — which is precisely why an indexer is load-bearing here and an eth_call is not.

Case 03 — World, and the market that shouldn't exist

An Orb enrolment costs an adversary about fifty cents to rent and hundreds of dollars to forge. We score the fifty. Every protocol that hardened, hardened against sale — user-presence checks, identity staking — and none against rental, because the human stays willing.

There is a counter-intuitive lesson buried in the credential black markets that followed World's 2023 launch: a protocol with no observable credential market may be further along the corruption curve, not safer. Idena beat trading, and the displaced demand reappeared as puppeteering. Hardening one channel prices the adjacent channel in.

“Illicit account trading in protocols should not be treated as evidence of advanced mechanisms or protections; to the contrary, illicit trading may signal a lack of them and be a precursor to puppeteering.” Ohlhaver, Nikulin & Berman — Compressed to 0 (2024)

How each sponsor's product is used here

Three integrations, one per sponsor. The test we held ourselves to was whether the product is load-bearing: remove it and this stops working, rather than losing a feature. Each panel below opens into the contracts, endpoints and source files, so none of this has to be taken on trust.

World

AgentKit — New Use Cases

A counterparty that refuses to trade with an agent unless a real human stands behind it — and refuses the second agent backed by that same human.

The new trust model
The brief asks where human-backed agents change access, authorization or rate limits. Our answer is a limit that cannot be expressed without personhood at all: a cap per human, not per key. Rate limits count requests; wallets are free; a bot farm simply brings more of them. Counting distinct humans behind the agents is the one budget an attacker cannot mint. Three agents, three outcomes — unregistered denied, human-backed allowed, and a third denied because it resolves to the same human as the second.
Meaningful use, not a wrapper
AgentKit runs on both sides. The agent uses createAgentkitClient; the counterparty is a real AgentKit resource server; the whole handshake executes live — request, HTTP 402, a signed CAIP-122 challenge, retry. Human backing is then read from AgentBook.
Open the implementation detail
  • The read. lookupHuman(address) on AgentBook, World Chain — 0xA23aB271…b944dA. A plain eth_call returning the Orb-verified human id for any address: no API key, no relying-party registration, no user interaction. That is why World is in the roster at all — every adapter here has to be readable without the issuer's cooperation, so that nothing we publish can be rate-limited or switched off later. adapters/world.ts
  • The fleet cap. AgentBook registration events are indexed chain-wide, so the limit is enforced against every agent a human has ever registered — not only the ones that happen to appear in a session. Without that sweep a farm just presents a fresh agent each time. fleet.ts
  • The handshake. A real AgentKit resource server, not a mock: request → HTTP 402 with the agentkit extension → signed CAIP-122 challenge → retry. One implementation note worth passing back — AgentKit compares the SIWE domain against the URL hostname while the payload carries host:port, so a dev server on a non-80 port is rejected with "Domain mismatch" unless you follow its convention. counterparty/server.js
  • Where World sits in the ontology. Three entries on three different trust roots — Orb (iris), document tier (passport chip), Selfie Check (liveness). The document tier deliberately shares its root with ZKPassport, Self and Rarimo, so a subject holding all four is counted once. That is the saturation rule applied to a sponsor's own product rather than around it.
  • Reproduce: cd apps/agent && npm start prints the verdict trace for all three agents.

The Graph

Best AI Use Case of The Graph

An agent that reasons about whether a person is real — over data no contract call can return.

Why an indexer is load-bearing, not decorative
A contract answers “does this credential exist”. It cannot answer when it was issued, whether it has since been lost, or where this account sits in a trust graph — and all three change the answer. Proof of Humanity took roughly 95% of its lifetime registrations inside one four-month airdrop window, so age is the signal there. Pull the subgraph and a vouched credential falls back to a flagged midpoint: we show that degradation on purpose rather than hiding it.
The AI half
An MCP server puts this in front of any agent as conversation: look up a subject, test a policy, explain the trust roots, and audit any weight's full history. The agent reasons over live indexed data; it is not reading a cached number.
The query only an indexer makes possible
As-of scoring. Score a subject by the rules and the credential state as they stood at a past block. That is what turns an audit trail from something you publish into something you can execute — “why did my score change” becomes a query with a block number in the answer.
Open the implementation detail
  • Protocol subgraph — Subgraph Studio, Gnosis: api.studio.thegraph.com/query/77602/poh. Supplies issuance dates, revocations, and Circles trust-graph position. Query { _meta { block { number } hasIndexingErrors } } to see it live.
  • Registry audit-trail subgraph — a self-hosted graph-node indexing our own contract: 37.27.67.44:8100. Every weight the ontology has ever assigned, each with the source it was derived from and the block it landed in. Two Graph products, consumed by one SDK.
  • What it indexes. The registry on Sepolia — 0x977b028b…60aa07, revision 44, 32 adapters over 19 trust roots. The counts on this page are read from that contract at page load, not typed into the copy.
  • Why an indexer and not an RPC. A contract answers whether a credential exists. It cannot say when it was issued, whether it has since been revoked, or where an account sits in a trust graph — and each of those changes the answer. Proof of Humanity (Gnosis) took roughly 95% of its lifetime registrations inside one four-month airdrop window, so on that registry age is the signal. Remove the subgraph and a vouched credential falls back to a flagged 0.5 midpoint — never to full weight, because otherwise making our indexer unreachable would be profitable. scoring.ts
  • The query only an indexer makes possible. As-of scoring: reconstruct the ontology and credential state at a past block, so an audit trail becomes executable rather than merely published. It refuses to degrade — with no registry subgraph configured it throws instead of answering a question about the past using today's weights. as-of.ts
  • The AI surface. An MCP server exposing this to any agent: look up a subject, test a policy, explain the trust roots, audit a weight's history. npx @printid/mcp. mcp/src/server.ts

ENS

Best ENS Integration for AI Agents

ENS as the place an agent says which human stands behind it — and the human says yes.

The gap we fill, in ENS's own drafts
ENS links two agent ENSIPs from this prize page. ENSIP-26 defines agent-context and agent-endpoint[mcp] — how to find an agent and how to talk to it. ENSIP-25 defines agent-registration — that a name controls a registry entry. Neither defines a binding between an agent and the human behind it. That is the record we added, and it is bidirectional: the agent's subname names its human, and the human's name acknowledges the agent back. One-directional would let anyone claim any human.
Why it is not cosmetic
Delete the records and the per-human fleet cap cannot be computed — there is nothing left that maps an agent to a person. ENS is the only place that mapping can live and still be user-controlled, on-chain, revocable, publicly readable and hosted by nobody. The alternative is a server of ours holding it, which is exactly the correlation this design exists to avoid.
Humans are plural too
A print.subjects text record lets a person declare their own set of wallets, which real people need: Proof of Humanity's own Circles proxy puts one person's credentials on two different addresses. Self-asserted, and the result says so — correlated credentials still saturate across the declared set, so listing more wallets cannot inflate anything.
Open the implementation detail
  • The name tree, live on Sepolia. print.eth with agent subnames alpha.print.eth and beta.print.eth — open it in the ENS app. Resolver 0xE99638b4…BBE49b5. Nothing is hard-coded: every field is read from the resolver at lookup time.
  • The binding is bidirectional, and that is the point. The agent's subname names its backing human; the human's name acknowledges that agent back. One direction alone would let anyone point a subname at a well-credentialled stranger and inherit their standing — so the counterparty reads both sides before it agrees to anything. ens-agents.ts
  • The gap, in ENS's own drafts. ENSIP-26 defines agent-context and agent-endpoint[<protocol>] — how to find an agent and how to talk to it — and specifies no human binding at all. ENSIP-25 attests that a name controls a registry entry, which is name-to-registration, not name-to-person. Both are drafts; the record described above is the piece neither covers.
  • Humans are plural too. An observer.print.subjects text record lets a person declare their own wallet set — which real people need, since Proof of Humanity's own Circles proxy puts one person's credentials on two different addresses. It is self-asserted and the result says so; correlated credentials still saturate across the declared set, so naming more wallets cannot inflate anything.

    The keys are reverse-dot Service Keys per ENSIP-5, namespaced under the domain we actually own rather than squatting a bare word in the global namespace. That is also the honest way to ask for a Global Key later: propose the standard, do not presume it.
